Sanctions diplomacy: US pressure, Canada retaliations and Syria delisting

Sanctions Diplomacy 2026-08-26 • 115 articles in total

In brief
  • The US is intensifying sanctions on Iran and escalating tariff conflicts with Canada, prompting international backlash and domestic strain in Iran.
  • Canada defends its industries through retaliation and diplomacy, while the US uses sanctions and tariffs as strategic tools to isolate adversaries and influence trade behavior.
  • The US removal of Syria from the terrorism list fosters regional optimism for diplomacy, amid ongoing global debates on sanctions enforcement and humanitarian impacts.

This batch of reporting shows two parallel sanctions dramas: an intensified US campaign of unilateral and secondary sanctions aimed chiefly at Iran, and a sharp tariff confrontation between the United States and Canada. Washington’s expanding financial and trade penalties — and threats to sanction third-party trading partners — have provoked strong pushback from China, regional Gulf states, and nations worried about extraterritorial reach. In Iran the measures are feeding immediate domestic strains (fuel queues, price and supply worries) and stern official threats of retaliation. Separately, the US removal of Syria from the State Sponsors of Terrorism list has been greeted positively across the region and by Syrian authorities, potentially opening space for diplomatic and economic engagement. Wider multilateral sanction activity and enforcement moves (arms embargo debates, designation changes, transit-risk listings) underline broader global tensions over using economic measures to pursue security and political objectives.

Washington signals willingness to apply secondary measures and public threats to broaden enforcement, framing these steps as central to national security and economic policy.

China and partners oppose US extraterritorial sanctions

Beijing and allied or closely aligned states publicly reject US threats of secondary sanctions, warning they will defend their commercial ties and may retaliate if Washington extends measures to third-party trade. These actors present the US approach as unlawful or destabilizing for global commerce and resistant to unilateral enforcement beyond national jurisdiction.
