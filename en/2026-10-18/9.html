Bukele's selective parole plan for gang collaborators

El Salvador Gang Parole Policy 2026-10-18 • 5 articles in total

In brief
  • El Salvador will review cases of occasional gang collaborators for early release but will not free active gang members.
  • The program offers benefits for detainees cooperating with public works or investigations while facing criticism over due process and safety.
  • Media reports vary by region, highlighting government clarifications, operational details, and human rights concerns.

President Nayib Bukele and his government have clarified they will not free half of El Salvador’s prison population but will review cases of thousands of detainees identified as occasional or ‘casual’ gang collaborators. Authorities describe a program that can grant early release or benefits to detainees who cooperate with public works or assist investigations, while insisting active gang members will not be released. Reporting varies on the numbers and specifics — some pieces cite reviews of roughly 10,000 cases and releases from the CECOT, others frame earlier claims as misrepresentations. Humanitarian groups and critics warn the policy risks applying to people not yet convicted and could undermine due process and public safety. The debate has domestic political implications as the administration balances security messaging with legal and human-rights scrutiny.

Countries covering this topic

Denial of mass-release claims

Spanish and Colombian outlets emphasize the government’s rebuttal of reports that Bukele planned to release half the prison population, presenting such claims as misrepresentations and clarifications from the presidency. These accounts note the proposal is framed as a case review of collaborators rather than a blanket amnesty, while still mentioning critics’ due-process concerns.

  • Bukele clarifies that he will not release half of the prisoners in El Salvador: it was a misrepresentation

Scope and numbers of planned releases

Costa Rican and Panamanian reporting focuses on the operational details and possible scale, noting announcements about releases from the CECOT and that roughly half of evaluated occasional collaborators could qualify for early release. These pieces stress the government line that benefits target casual collaborators and exclude active gang members, and highlight the political implications of the program’s scope.

  • Bukele reveals how many prisoners will be released from the CECOT
  • No gang member will be released: Bukele clarifies parole plan

Program defended; NGOs warn of rights risks

Nicaraguan coverage centers on the government defense of a scheme granting early release in exchange for public works and cooperation, while humanitarian NGOs caution it may apply to detainees who have not been convicted. This perspective foregrounds concerns that the policy could undermine justice and public safety despite official framing as rehabilitative or cooperative measures.
