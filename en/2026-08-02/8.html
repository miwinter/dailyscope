US–Cuba Diplomacy: Accusations and Competing Narratives

US–Cuba Diplomacy 2026-08-02 • 5 articles in total

In brief
  • U.S. sources depict Cuba as an adversary conducting influence operations and supporting protests within the United States.
  • Cuba views U.S. policy as coercive historical and ongoing domination and calls for cooperation based on mutual respect.

Recent coverage reveals sharply divided narratives that complicate US–Cuba diplomacy. U.S. sources portray Cuba as an adversary and allege Havana supports influence operations and protests inside the United States, linking it to broader anti-American networks. Cuban commentary rejects that portrayal, framing U.S. policy as long-standing coercion—rooted in practices like the Platt Amendment and contemporary "gunboat diplomacy"—and calls instead for cooperation based on mutual respect. These conflicting threat perceptions and historical grievances constrain prospects for constructive bilateral engagement and shape regional policy debates.

U.S. security and influence concerns

U.S. officials and commentators characterize Cuba as an adversary involved in influence operations and regional anti-American networks, citing ties to actors like Iran and Russia. Allegations include claims that Cuba finances or organizes protests and uses networks to pressure U.S. policymakers, framing the issue as a security and foreign-policy challenge.

Cuban critique of U.S. coercion and call for respectful cooperation

Cuban sources portray American policy as a structural pattern of coercion—described as "gunboat diplomacy" and historically exemplified by the Platt Amendment—that undermines Cuban sovereignty. They reject accusations of hostile influence, emphasize resistance to external tutelage, and argue that cooperation rooted in mutual respect would better serve both peoples and global norms.
