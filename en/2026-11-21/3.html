Great Power Rivalry

Great Power Rivalry 2026-11-21 • 36 articles in total

In brief
  • The US, Russia, and China compete via diplomacy, military signaling, and economic influence, causing alliance strains and regional security challenges.
  • Moscow stresses sanction resilience and military means; Beijing uses economic and tech leverage; Trump-era US ties affect perceptions and deterrence planning.
  • Smaller states assert autonomy and warn summit optics can't replace local realities; new weapons and covert tactics reshape deterrence and ally responses.

The United States, Russia and China are jockeying for influence through a mix of personal diplomacy, strategic signaling, and material power projection—creating frictions in alliances and regional security. Personal ties and summit theatrics have altered perceptions of U.S. reliability, while Moscow stresses resilience under sanction and Beijing leverages economic and technological reach. Nuclear and advanced-weapons signaling, from briefcases to kamikaze drones, elevates risks and forces allies to reassess deterrence and contingency planning. Smaller states and frontline actors stress agency and warn that summit optics cannot replace local realities in resolving conflicts.

Trump’s personalized diplomacy and its fallout

Former President Trump’s public cultivation of personal ties with Xi and Putin reshapes the narrative of great-power competition, creating both openings and anxieties about U.S. reliability. His comments and actions—on friendships, nuclear posture and crisis signalling—have pushed European and regional partners to rethink deterrence and contingency planning.
